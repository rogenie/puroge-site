## Read this if your closet is full and you still have nothing to wear

You buy good things. You don’t mind paying for quality. And yet the blazer you wore to a client meeting last week felt like cling film by noon, and the “easy” travel pants you bought for a trip pilled before the second wash.

You’re not imagining it. Clothing changed, quietly, over the last twenty years. This page explains how, what it costs the person inside the clothes, and the one rule that puts it right.

It is long because the answer deserves it. If you only have a minute, read [the fiber rule](#the-fiber-rule).

## The problem nobody put on the label

Look at the label on what you’re wearing right now. Polyester. Nylon. Acrylic. Elastane.

These are plastics. Polyester is the same family of plastic used for drink bottles. It is made from oil, spun into thread, and woven into the clothes most of us wear against our skin for sixteen hours a day.

That alone wouldn’t matter much if the clothes were good. They often aren’t, and the numbers show it.

- **We buy far more and wear it far less.** Between 2000 and 2015, clothing production roughly doubled while the average number of times a garment is worn before it is thrown away fell by 36%.[^emf]
- **It goes straight to landfill.** Worldwide, the equivalent of one garbage truck of textiles is landfilled or burned every second. Less than 1% of the material used to make clothing is recycled into new clothing.[^emf]
- **America is no exception.** U.S. landfills received 11.3 million tons of textiles in 2018, and only about 15% of textiles were recycled.[^epa]

## It gets closer to home than the landfill

The landfill is far away. Your skin isn’t.

**Every wash sheds plastic.** The International Union for Conservation of Nature estimates that washing synthetic clothes accounts for about 35% of the primary microplastics released into the world’s oceans.[^iucn] Those fibers don’t disappear. They move through water, food and air.

**Some of it ends up in us.** In 2022, researchers in the Netherlands tested the blood of 22 healthy adults and found measurable plastic particles in 17 of them, about 77%. The most common type was PET, the plastic used for bottles and polyester clothing.[^blood]

> We don’t yet know what plastic in the blood does to the body. We would rather not wear more of it while we wait to find out.

To be clear about the evidence: scientists have not shown that wearing polyester causes illness, and the blood study does not say where the particles came from. What it does show is that plastic is now inside people, and clothing is one of the largest sources of loose plastic fiber we know of.

**It also simply feels worse.** Synthetic fabric doesn’t absorb moisture the way plant fibers do, so on a long day it holds sweat and warmth against you. Anyone who has worn a polyester blouse through a summer commute knows the feeling.

## It isn’t your fault

Purogé is named for two grandmothers, Lola Purisima and Lola Roberta, who sewed and sold clothes for a living. In their world, a garment was judged by its seams and how many years it lasted.

That world didn’t disappear because shoppers got careless. The industry was rebuilt around volume. Plastic fiber is cheap, so clothes got cheaper, so more of them were made, so each one was designed to be worn less. Even expensive labels quietly moved to synthetic blends.

So if your closet is full of pieces that don’t breathe and don’t last, that is the system working as designed. The fix isn’t to feel guilty. It is to change one habit.

## The fiber rule

<div class="rule" markdown="1">
<p class="label">The fiber rule</p>

Wear plants against your skin. Buy fewer pieces, and buy the ones that will outlast the trend.
</div>

Before you buy anything, ask three questions:

1. **What is it made of?** Look for plant or animal fiber at the top of the label: linen, cotton, hemp, wool, silk. Treat a synthetic blend as a compromise, not a default.
2. **Will it breathe?** If you will wear it all day, at work or on a plane, choose the fiber that lets heat and moisture out.
3. **Will I still wear it in ten years?** Good construction, a quiet color and a cut that suits you will do more for the planet than any eco label.

## Why linen comes first

If you only change one fabric in your wardrobe, change to linen. Here is why.

- **It is grown, not refined.** Linen comes from the flax plant. Flax can often be grown on rainfall alone and needs significantly less fertilizer and pesticide than cotton.[^cfda]
- **It is built to last.** Flax fibers grow up to 90 centimeters long, compared with about 3.5 centimeters for cotton. Long fibers make strong yarn. Well-cared-for linen can last up to three decades.[^cfda][^goy]
- **It handles heat and moisture.** Flax is stronger than cotton and dries faster,[^cfda] which is why linen has been the warm-weather fabric of choice for centuries.
- **It gets better with wear.** Linen softens with every wash instead of pilling.
- **It sheds no plastic.** Pure linen is a plant fiber. Check the label: a “linen blend” with polyester gives some of this back.

## Two pieces do most of the work

Most people wear a small handful of pieces most of the time. So start there.

For the people we dress, those pieces are a **linen top** and a **comfortable pair of pants**. Together they cover the client meeting, the long-haul flight and the dinner after. One good top and one good pair of pants, worn often and cared for properly, replace a drawer full of things that never felt right.

<div class="callout" markdown="1">
<p class="label">The arithmetic of buying well</p>

An illustration, not a promise: a $180 linen top worn 100 times costs $1.80 per wear. A $30 synthetic top worn 7 times before it pills or stretches costs about $4.29 per wear, and then you buy it again.
</div>

## This isn’t for everyone

Linen is honest about what it is. It creases. It looks lived-in by the afternoon. If you want every outfit to look freshly pressed at 6 p.m., or you buy a new outfit for every event, natural fiber will frustrate you.

But if you would rather own fewer, better pieces that feel good on your skin and age well, you will wonder why you waited.

## What to do next

1. Open your closet and check the labels on the five things you wear most.
2. Pick the one that bothers you most, usually a top or a pair of work pants.
3. Replace it with a natural-fiber piece you will wear for years.

<p class="ps"><strong>P.S.</strong> You don’t have to replace everything. You only have to stop buying plastic for the pieces that touch your skin the most. Start with the top and the pants.</p>
