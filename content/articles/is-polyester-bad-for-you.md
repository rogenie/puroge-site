## Read this if your favorite blouse feels fine at nine and clammy by three

You didn’t choose polyester. It chose you.

It’s in the blouse that never needs ironing, the “performance” trousers, the lining of the good blazer. You’ve noticed it holds a smell after one wear. You’ve wondered whether wearing plastic all day is quietly doing something.

This is a straight answer to that question. What the research shows, what it doesn’t, and how to decide what touches your skin.

## What is polyester, actually?

Polyester is plastic. Most of it is PET, which the CFDA describes as the same type of plastic used in soda bottles.[^cfda] It is usually made from petroleum, melted, extruded into filament and spun into thread.[^cfda]

It earned its place honestly. It is durable, cheap, resists wrinkles and holds its color in sunlight.[^cfda]

And it is now most of what the world wears. Textile Exchange’s Materials Market Report 2025 found that polyester made up 59% of all fiber produced in 2024, about 78 million tonnes, and synthetics overall reached 69%.[^te]

So the question isn’t niche. It is about the majority of the clothes in most closets.

## Is polyester bad for you? The honest problem

Here is the short version: polyester is not known to be acutely harmful to wear, and science has not shown that it causes illness.

That is the real answer, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

But “not proven harmful” is not the same as “good to wear against your skin for sixteen hours.” There are four things the research does show.

## What the evidence shows

**It holds less moisture.** The CFDA lists polyester as having low absorbency.[^cfda] On a long day, sweat that a plant fiber would take up tends to sit between the fabric and your skin instead. That is the clammy feeling at three o’clock.

**It tends to hold odor.** In a 2014 study at Ghent University, 26 adults wore cotton or synthetic shirts during a spinning class. After 28 hours sealed in bags, a panel rated the polyester shirts smellier than the cotton ones, and odor-linked *Micrococcus* bacteria were more common on the polyester.[^odor] The researchers described the study as preliminary.[^odor] Still, it matches what most people notice in their own laundry.

**Its dyes can bother some skin.** Polyester is usually colored with disperse dyes, a class DermNet lists for synthetic fibers such as polyester and acrylic.[^dermnet] Certain disperse blue dyes are common causes of contact dermatitis, and sweat can help dyes leach out of fabric.[^dermnet] In a study of 858 patch-tested patients in Sweden and Belgium, about 18% suspected textiles of causing their skin problems, and synthetic materials were the textiles most often blamed.[^lund] This affects some people, not everyone. But if your skin flares at the waistband, the fabric is worth a look.

**It sheds plastic every wash.** CFDA notes that polyester sheds microplastics with every wash.[^cfda] Researchers at the University of Plymouth estimated that a 6 kg load of polyester can release about 496,000 fibers. Acrylic released even more, about 729,000.[^plymouth]

> Polyester isn’t poison. It’s plastic. Those are different claims, and both are worth knowing.

## Where the evidence stops

Microplastics are now inside people. In 2022, researchers at VU Amsterdam tested the blood of 22 donors and found plastic particles in about three-quarters of them. PET, the plastic in most polyester, was the type detected most often.[^vu]

Now the careful part.

The study does not say where those particles came from. Bottles, packaging, dust and food are all candidates, as well as clothing. And the researchers were clear that the health effects are not yet known and need more research.[^vu]

So: plastic is in us, polyester is one of the largest sources of loose plastic fiber, and what that means for health is still being studied. That is everything the evidence supports today. We won’t stretch it further.

## It isn’t your fault

If most of your closet is polyester, you didn’t make a mistake.

The industry made a choice. Synthetic fiber is cheap to produce at scale, and its share of global output keeps growing.[^te] Polyester shows up in the price points, the “easy care” promises and the linings of clothes that look natural from the outside.

You were never shown the label first. Now you can be.

## The new way: the skin rule

<div class="rule" markdown="1">
<p class="label">The skin rule</p>

What touches your skin all day should be a plant or animal fiber. Plastic can stay on the outside.
</div>

You don’t need to throw anything away. A polyester raincoat over a linen shirt is fine. A wool coat with a synthetic lining is fine.

The rule only governs the layer against your body: the blouse, the shirt, the trousers you sit in through a long meeting or a long flight.

## Why the rule works

It targets the four problems directly.

- **Moisture.** Plant fibers take up moisture instead of trapping it against you.
- **Odor.** In the Ghent tests, cotton supported very few odor-causing bacteria, while polyester supported continued growth.[^odor]
- **Skin.** DermNet’s practical advice for people sensitive to textile dyes is to choose light-colored natural fabrics such as cotton, linen and wool, and to wash new clothes at least three times before wearing them.[^dermnet]
- **Shedding.** A linen shirt sheds no plastic, because it contains none. Note that blends give some of that back: in the Plymouth study, even a polyester-cotton blend released roughly 138,000 fibers per load.[^plymouth]

<div class="callout" markdown="1">
<p class="label">Read the label, not the look</p>

“Linen-look,” “silky” and “cotton-feel” describe a finish, not a fiber. The fiber content is on the care label inside the side seam. If it says 100% linen, cotton or wool, it passes the skin rule.
</div>

## What to replace first

Not everything. Start with the pieces that spend the most hours against you.

For most people who dress for work and travel, that is two things: a top and a pair of trousers. A linen top that breathes through a full day of meetings. Linen pants you can wear on a twelve-hour flight and still take to dinner.

Those two pieces cover more skin-hours than anything else in the closet. Replace them once, in natural fiber, and you’ve applied the skin rule where it matters most.

## Who this isn’t for

If you run marathons in technical kit, keep it. Synthetics are built for that job.

If you have a diagnosed skin condition, this article is not medical advice. That conversation belongs with a dermatologist.

And if you love a polyester piece and wear it often, keep wearing it. The most sustainable garment is the one you already own. The skin rule is for what you buy next.

## What to do next

Tonight, check the labels on the top and trousers you wear most. If either says polyester, that is your first replacement. Choose linen, and wash it before the first wear.

<p class="ps"><strong>P.S.</strong> The research doesn’t say polyester will make you ill. It says polyester is plastic, it sheds, and it sits against your skin all day. That is reason enough to choose what touches you.</p>
