## Read this if you've ever stood in a sunny doorway and wondered what everyone could see

You found a pair of linen trousers you loved. Then you caught yourself in a shop window at noon and saw more than you meant to.

Or you have been eyeing white linen for months and haven't bought it, because you can't tell from a product photo whether it will be sheer.

It is a fair worry. And it has a precise answer.

## Is linen see-through?

Sometimes. Linen isn't see-through by nature. Some linen is woven to be sheer, and most isn't.

Linen is a family of cloths, not one fabric. At one end sits linen gauze, which Sartor Bohemia describes as a loosely woven, transparent fabric of 80 to 90 GSM, meant for layering, scarves and curtains.[^sartor] At the other end, heavy linen over 200 GSM is used for blazers.[^sartor]

So the honest question isn't "is linen see-through?" It is "is *this* linen see-through, for *this* garment?"

## The problem: weight is only part of the story

Most people look for one number. Usually that's GSM, grams per square metre, which measures fabric weight as mass per unit area.[^nooteboom]

Weight helps. It doesn't settle it.

Nooteboom Textiles puts it plainly: a higher GSM may improve coverage in otherwise comparable fabrics, but it does not guarantee opacity.[^nooteboom] Yarn spacing, color, structure and finishing all influence how much light passes through.[^nooteboom]

Three things decide whether you can see through a piece of linen:

**Weight.** A lighter fabric has more drape and is sheerer, and a heavier one is thicker and sturdier.[^fstore]

**Density.** Cornell's apparel business guide notes that yarn diameter and the number of yarns per inch determine a fabric's weight.[^cornell] The same two things decide how much open space is left between yarns. Fabrics-Store sets out the logic: fine thread at a high thread count can still be opaque, while fine thread at a low count is see-through. Even a thick thread at a low count can let light through.[^fstore]

**Color.** Light colors may look more transparent than dark colors at the same GSM.[^canvas] A pale, open-weave linen can look more transparent than a darker or tighter one of similar weight.[^canvas]

## Why it catches people out

A product page that says "100% linen" tells you the fiber. It tells you nothing about the cloth.

A 120 GSM batiste and a 220 GSM trouser linen are both, accurately, "100% linen." One is made for a breezy summer blouse. The other is made for walking into a client meeting.

And the conditions you test in are rarely the conditions you wear in. Fitting rooms are lit from above. Sunlight comes from behind you, at an angle, outdoors.

## It isn't your fault

Fast fashion has trained all of us to buy by photo and by fiber name. Weight is often missing from the listing entirely, and thread count, as Canvas ETC notes, is not the same measure as GSM and can't substitute for it.[^canvas]

So when a lightweight piece turns out to be sheer, the fabric isn't failing. It's doing what it was made for, in a job it wasn't made for. Nobody told you the weight.

## The new way: the window test

<div class="rule" markdown="1">
<p class="label">The window test</p>

Hold one layer to daylight with your hand behind it. If you can see your fingers, so can everyone else.
</div>

Thirty seconds at a window or under a lamp tells you more than any label. Do it with a single layer, not a folded piece, and look at the color you will actually wear.

## Which linen weight for which garment?

Weight categories aren't official standards. Every guide below calls its ranges rough guidance. They are useful as a starting point, though, and they broadly agree.

<div class="callout" markdown="1">
<p class="label">Linen weights, roughly</p>

**Very light, about 80–120 GSM:** described by Canvas ETC as very light, fluid and often sheer.[^canvas] Scarves, layers, curtains.

**Light, about 120–160 GSM:** warm-weather shirts and blouses, and may show light through.[^canvas] de Linum puts loose tops, summer shirts and light dresses at 90–140 GSM.[^delinum]

**Light-medium to medium, about 140–230 GSM:** de Linum suggests 140–180 GSM for dresses, shirts and relaxed trousers, and 180–230 GSM for trousers, skirts and structured dresses.[^delinum]

**Heavy, over 200 GSM:** blazers and structured pieces.[^sartor]

To convert, divide GSM by 33.9 to get ounces per square yard.[^canvas]
</div>

Cornell's guide makes the general point simply: a shirt or blouse will typically use a lighter fabric than trousers or a skirt.[^cornell]

The specific warning worth remembering comes from Canvas ETC: a lightweight linen that works for a relaxed shirt may be too sheer for unlined trousers.[^canvas]

## Why the window test works

Light passes through the gaps between yarns. Textile engineers measure how much of a fabric's surface the yarns actually cover, a figure called the cover factor, and a study of woven constructions notes that it influences properties including opacity.[^tl]

Weight and coverage usually move together, but not always. A closely woven fabric and a loosely made one can share a GSM and still differ in surface structure.[^nooteboom] Holding the cloth to the light measures coverage directly. No number on a label does that.

> The label tells you the fiber. The window tells you the cloth.

## What to wear when you want linen without the worry

For a working wardrobe, start with a linen top and a pair of linen pants chosen by weight, not just by fiber. Let the top be lighter, where a little air is the point. Let the trousers be medium weight or lined, so they hold their line and keep their privacy in a bright lobby or on a jet bridge.

If you love white linen, buy it a little heavier than you think, or lined. de Linum notes that very light linen may have some transparency, especially in pale colors, and that lining or careful underwear choices may be needed.[^delinum]

## Who this isn't for

If you want sheer linen, for a beach cover-up, a layering shirt or a summer scarf, you don't need a heavier weight. You want the gauze.

And if you never wear pale colors and always buy lined trousers, you've already solved most of this.

## What to do next

Before you buy your next linen piece, find the weight on the listing. If it isn't listed, ask. When it arrives, hold one layer to the window, in daylight, before you cut the tags.

<p class="ps"><strong>P.S.</strong> Linen isn't see-through. Light, open, pale linen is. Choose the weight for the job and check it against the light.</p>
