## Read this if you've ever landed feeling damp, cold and creased at the same time

The flight is eleven hours. You boarded in the clothes you'll wear to your first meeting, or your first dinner.

By hour three you're too warm. By hour six you've got the blanket up to your chin. By the time you land, the "travel" outfit you bought for this trip is clinging in some places and stiff in others.

That isn't bad luck. It's a fabric question with a clear answer.

## What makes a long flight hard on clothes?

A long-haul cabin is an unusual place to spend half a day.

**The air is very dry.** The UK Civil Aviation Authority says cabin air has about 20% humidity, compared with roughly 40 to 60% in an office.[^caa] The 2005 edition of *International Travel and Health*, prepared with the aviation bodies ICAO and IATA, put it at usually less than 20%.[^who] Both say it's normal for skin, lips and eyes to feel drier after a flight.[^caa][^who]

**The temperature moves.** In a NIOSH-collected study of 33 flights on 10 aircraft types, average cabin temperatures ranged from 22.4 °C to 28.2 °C from gate to gate. Five-minute readings ran from 19 °C to 29.5 °C.[^niosh] Humidity was lowest on long flights.[^niosh] That data is from 2000, and cabins vary by aircraft and airline. But the pattern most travelers know holds: warm at boarding, cool over the ocean at night.

**You can't change.** Whatever touches your skin at the gate is still there when you land.

So the right fabric for a long flight has three jobs. It has to handle your own warmth and moisture. It has to work across a range of temperatures. And it has to still feel, and smell, decent after twelve hours.

## Why the usual "travel" clothes let you down

Many travel clothes are sold on one promise: they don't wrinkle. That's usually because they're synthetic.

Wrinkle resistance is real. It's also the least important thing on hour eleven.

The problem with an all-synthetic outfit isn't safety. It's comfort over time. A study from Ghent University, published in *Applied and Environmental Microbiology* in 2014, had 26 people do an intensive, hour-long spinning session and then compared their shirts.[^asm] The polyester shirts smelled worse than the cotton ones.[^asm] The researchers found that micrococci, the main odor-causing bacteria they identified on the clothes, grew better on polyester.[^sd]

A flight is not a spin class. But you're in the same clothes for longer, in a warm, crowded space, and any odor has a long time to build.

## It isn't your fault

If your travel wardrobe is mostly synthetic, that's what the market offered you.

"Performance" and "wrinkle-free" are easy to put on a tag. "Breathes well when the cabin is warm, and still smells fine on landing" is harder to sell in four words. So most of us were taught to shop for the wrong problem.

You don't need to replace everything. You need to know what to reach for next.

Natural fiber against the skin, one natural layer you can take off, and nothing tight.

## Which fabrics actually work on a long flight?

### Linen: the base layer for warm cabins and warm destinations

Linen is made from flax. The CFDA, the Council of Fashion Designers of America, describes flax as similar to cotton chemically but faster drying, with good thermo-regulating properties: cool in hot weather, warm in cool.[^cfda]

It also takes up moisture. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston's materials database lists linen's moisture regain at 12%.[^cameo] Moisture regain is how much water a fiber holds from the air under standard conditions, and it's a rough guide to how well a fabric handles sweat.

The catch is creasing. The CFDA notes flax wrinkles easily.[^cfda] On a plane, that's a cosmetic problem, not a comfort one. A relaxed cut in navy, olive or tobacco hides most of it. (Our guide to [keeping linen from wrinkling](/journal/how-to-keep-linen-from-wrinkling/) covers the rest.)

### Cotton: the familiar, comfortable middle

A soft cotton top or a cotton knit is a good base layer too. It's breathable, it's what most people already own, and in the Ghent study it held onto less odor than polyester.[^asm] It dries more slowly than linen.[^cfda] Choose a looser weave over a dense, heavy jersey.

### Wool: the layer for the cold part of the flight

Fine merino isn't just for winter. The Woolmark Company, the wool industry's research body, says wool can absorb up to 35% of its weight in moisture before it feels wet.[^woolmark] It also says wool's structure absorbs odors and releases them only when washed.[^woolmark] Those are industry figures, so weigh them that way. But they match what makes wool useful in a cabin: it keeps you warm when the air cools, and it doesn't get stale.

A thin wool sweater or cardigan over a linen or cotton top covers the whole flight.

### Synthetics: fine in small doses

A little stretch in a waistband or a nylon travel bag is no problem. The issue is a full outfit of polyester worn for twelve hours, where odor has more time to build.[^asm]

## What does a good long-flight outfit look like?

You don't need a special travel wardrobe. You need a formula.

- **Top:** a loose linen or cotton shirt or tee.
- **Pants:** relaxed linen or soft cotton trousers with a comfortable waist. Linen pants are breathable over a long sit and look right on arrival.
- **Layer:** a fine merino or cashmere knit, or a wool cardigan.
- **Feet:** cotton or wool socks, and shoes that slip on and off.
- **Extra:** a large scarf in wool or linen. It's a blanket, a pillow and a cover-up.

They breathe through the warm part of the flight, dry quickly if you spill water on them, and look intentional when you land. Add the wool layer over the top, take it off when you're warm, and one outfit covers gate, cabin and the first dinner.

If the trip ends in a meeting or a dinner, wear the pants you'll arrive in and pack the top you'll change into. That's one fresh piece, not a full change.

## Who this isn't for

If you fly an hour or two at a time, wear whatever you like. Fabric matters most when you can't change for half a day.

If you need a garment for a specific medical reason, follow your doctor's advice, not a fabric guide. Nothing here is a health claim. Clothing can make a flight more comfortable; it doesn't change the air you breathe.

And if you love your synthetic travel pieces, keep them. Try swapping just the layer against your skin next time and see how you feel on landing.

## What to do before your next long flight

Lay out the outfit you'd normally fly in. Check the labels.

If the piece touching your skin is polyester, swap it for linen or cotton. If you have no warm layer, add a fine wool one. Loosen anything tight.

That's it. Three changes, all of them reversible.

<p class="ps"><strong>P.S.</strong> The cabin will be dry and its temperature will change. You can't control either. You can choose a fabric that breathes and a layer you can take off.</p>
